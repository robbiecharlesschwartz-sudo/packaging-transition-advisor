# Baseline (no retrieval): cosmetics-bottle

_Model: gemini-3.5-flash · same profile as the cited example · no documents, no citations, no verification_

## Summary

This transition brief evaluates sustainable packaging alternatives for a US nationwide cosmetics brand producing 2,000,000 units annually of 250 ml shampoo and body wash in rigid PET bottles (28g). Evaluating 100% PCR rPET, glass, aluminum, and reuse/refill models against functional needs (tamper-evident liquid containment) and sourcing constraints (US-only suppliers), 100% rPET emerges as the optimal immediate solution. It maintains existing filling line compatibility, dramatically lowers carbon footprint, and complies with upcoming state-level Recycled Content mandates without severe freight cost penalties.

## Alternatives shortlist

1. 100% Post-Consumer Recycled PET (rPET): 28g bottle. Direct drop-in replacement. Preserves shatter-resistance and compatible with standard tamper-evident screw caps/flip-tops.
2. Infinitely Recyclable Aluminum: 250 ml threaded aluminum bottle (~30g). Highly durable, premium feel, but requires specialized lining (BPA-NI) to prevent corrosion from surfactants.
3. Flint Glass: 250 ml glass bottle (~180g-220g). High-end aesthetic, inert, but introduces significant transit weight, energy intensity, and breakage risk in showers.
4. Reuse/Refill (Parent Bottle + Concentrate/Pouch): Durable aluminum or thick PET main bottle paired with lightweight flexible refill pouches or concentrated pods. Requires consumer behavior shift.

## Regulatory exposure by US state

1. California: Rigid Plastic Packaging Container (RPPC) law and SB 54 (Extended Producer Responsibility) requiring 30% recycled content by 2028 and 100% recyclable/compostable packaging by 2032. Eco-modulated EPR fees take effect in 2027.
2. Oregon: Plastic Pollution and Recycling Modernization Act (effective 2025) imposes EPR fees on producers based on packaging material recyclability.
3. Washington: Senate Bill 5022 mandates minimum post-consumer recycled content in household and personal care plastic bottles (15% by 2025, 25% by 2028, 50% by 2031).
4. New Jersey & Maine: Recycled content mandates (NJ S2515 requires 15% PCR in plastic containers by 2024, scaling to 50%) and active EPR program implementation through 2026.

## End-of-life reality check

1. rPET: PET (RIC #1) has the highest curbside access in the US (~87%). US mechanical recycling yields are good, though colored PET faces yield loss compared to clear PET.
2. Aluminum: Highest economic value in the US Municipal Solid Waste stream. Curbside sorting via eddy current separators achieves >50% real recovery rate for bottles.
3. Glass: Highly recyclable theoretically, but actual US recycling rate is ~31%. High weight increases MRF sorting breakages and transportation emissions to cullet processors.
4. Reuse/Refill: Refill pouches (multi-layer laminate) are currently non-recyclable in standard curbside systems and end up in landfills unless store drop-off programs are utilized.

## Green-claims risk

1. FTC Green Guides Compliance: Claims like '100% Recyclable' must be qualified if local recycling facilities are not available to at least 60% of consumers where sold.
2. California AB 1201 / SB 343 ('Chasing Arrows' Law): Prohibits use of the chasing arrows symbol or 'recyclable' claims unless proven that the material is routinely collected and processed into recycled feedstock in CA MRFs.
3. Deceptive Marketing Risk: Claims of 'Zero Waste' via refill pouches can trigger greenwashing lawsuits if the pouch itself is non-recyclable plastic.

## Supply chain and performance

1. Line Compatibility: rPET requires zero adjustments to existing liquid filling, capping, and tamper-evident neck banding equipment. Glass and aluminum require modified capping chucks, bottle handling starwheels, and line speed reductions due to denting/shattering.
2. Freight Impact: Glass adds ~700% mass compared to 28g PET, severely increasing outbound e-commerce and retail pallet shipping costs and Scope 3 emissions.
3. Barrier Properties: Surfactants and fragrances in shampoo/body wash can degrade unlined metal or cause stress-cracking; aluminum requires verified internal epoxy or polyolefin coatings.

## Rough cost comparison (per unit)

1. Virgin PET (Current Baseline): $0.12 - $0.16 per unit.
2. 100% rPET (28g): $0.16 - $0.21 per unit (+25-35% premium over virgin due to US rPET resin demand).
3. Threaded Aluminum (250 ml, lined): $0.45 - $0.65 per unit (+200-300% premium).
4. Glass Bottle (250 ml): $0.35 - $0.55 per unit (+150-250% premium, excluding increased shipping cost).
5. Reuse/Refill (Durable parent bottle + pouch): Parent bottle $1.20-$2.00; Refill pouch $0.18-$0.28 per unit.

## Market and competitor context

Personal care brands are aggressively moving away from virgin plastic. Unilever's Dove transitioned liquid wash bottles to 100% rPET across North America. Premium niche players like Plaine Products have successfully scaled full loop aluminum refill models, while mass brands like Native and Bath & Body Works are launching refill pouches and 100% PCR bottles to protect market share against tightening retail sustainability scorecards (e.g., Target Forward, Walmart Project Gigaton).
